# Document Story: Frank Nadvornik’s Copyright Papers

**A narrative of the documents found in a family envelope, placed in the historical environment of Czech-American communities in North Dakota and Oregon.**

**Last updated:** 2026-11-15

## Overview

While cleaning up a family property after a relative’s passing, an outer envelope from the U.S. Copyright Office was discovered. Inside it was a second envelope and a collection of materials spanning roughly 1921 to 1946: registration certificates for musical works, official circulars, a handwritten annotation, and a Czech-language booklet on natural healing. Together these papers trace the life and creative activity of **Frank Nadvornik** (also spelled Nadvorník), a Czech-American musician, composer, and naturopath who moved from Dickinson, North Dakota, to Scio, Oregon.

The documents reveal two distinct periods of activity and two communities shaped by Czech immigration.

## Timeline of the Documents

### Dickinson, North Dakota (early 1920s – mid-1920s)

- **14 February 1921** — Publication of *Československý Bílý Pochod* (Czechoslovak White March). Copies received 21 February. Registration Class E, XXc, No. 503142. Owner: Frank Nadvornik, Dickinson, N. Dak. Register: Thorvald Solberg.
- **6 March 1921** — Publication of *Česká Družba March* (Czech Friendship March), copyright claimed on the arrangement. Registration Class E, XXc, No. 505731.
- **7 March 1926** — Publication of *Ave Mundi* (words by Joseph Horpesty, music by Frank Nadvornik). Registration Class E, XXc, No. 473682.

These early certificates were found with the inner envelope. The same period produced the Czech booklet *Přírodní Léčení* (Natural Healing), which lists the author as Frank Nadvorník, Naturopath, Box 333, Dickinson, N.D.

Public Records System indexing later associated related 1921 registrations with the title *Česko-Slovenský Zběh, Pochod / Czecho-Slovak Deserter March* (E 503142 and E 505931), suggesting possible alternate cataloging or additional related works.

### Transition and Correspondence

- **July 1930** — Circular No. 37 (“Copyright in Foreign Countries”) preserved among the papers.
- **2 January 1934** — Letter from the Acting Register of Copyrights discussing rights and international copyright.
- **July 1940** — Circular No. 58 (Music) explaining registration procedures for unpublished and published musical works. A handwritten note later added to this circular records that $2.00 was remitted and two copies were sent on 18 April 1941 for *God Save America*.

### Scio, Oregon (1940s)

- **8 January 1941** — Outer envelope from the Library of Congress Copyright Office postmarked to Frank Nadvornik, Scio, Oregon (related to Class E pub. 94647).
- **18 April 1941** — Handwritten annotation on Circular No. 58 regarding the remittance and copies for *God Save America*.
- **24 April 1941** — Copies of *God Save America* received by the Copyright Office.
- **1 May 1941** — Publication date of *God Save America* (words and music by Frank Nadvornik). Registration Class E pub. No. 94647. Claimant: Frank Nadvornik, Scio, Oregon.
- **24 September 1946** — Publication of *Christmas Chants* / *Vánoční zpěvy* (bilingual arrangement). Registration EP-8701. Claimant/publisher: Frank Nadvornik, Scio, Oregon. Instrumentation noted as SATB chorus, piano, violin 1, violin 2, clarinet, trumpet.

An obituary later identified Frank Nadvornik as having been born 7 November 1877 in Czechoslovakia and dying in 1973 at age 95 in Saratoga, California, after having lived in Scio, Oregon.

## Historical Environment

### Dickinson, North Dakota, and the Czech Settlement

By 1900, roughly 130 Czech families lived on farms near Dickinson, making it the third-largest Czech settlement in North Dakota. Many of these families had not come directly from Bohemia or Moravia; a substantial number had lived for a generation or more in Crimea (then part of the Russian Empire) before emigrating. The nearby community of New Hradec became a focal point, with the Czech Catholic church of Saints Peter and Paul. Czech fraternal organizations such as the Western Czech Brotherhood United lodge were active. In the broader Great Plains, Czechs were among the largest continental European immigrant groups, with significant concentrations in North Dakota, Nebraska, Texas, and Kansas. Music, patriotic marches, and community gatherings were common ways of maintaining cultural identity in the years immediately after the founding of Czechoslovakia in 1918.

The early works registered by Nadvornik—Czech-titled marches and a piece with Latin title—fit this setting. The concurrent publication of a Czech-language naturopathy booklet suggests he was also practicing or promoting natural healing within the same community.

### Scio, Oregon, and Czech Settlement in the Willamette Valley

Scio, a small agricultural town in Linn County on Thomas Creek, was incorporated in 1866 and named after Scio, Ohio. By the 1930s the surrounding area had become one of the larger Czech communities on the Pacific Coast, with some accounts describing roughly 200 families. Czech fraternal halls (Z.C.B.J. lodges) served as social centers; dances were regularly advertised and open to the wider community. The town’s economy centered on farming, with later growth in timber and milling. Population figures show modest growth through the 1940s (approximately 351 in 1940, rising afterward). Nadvornik’s registration of *God Save America* in 1941 and the bilingual Christmas arrangement in 1946 occurred while he was living in this environment.

### Broader Context

The papers also reflect ordinary interactions with the U.S. Copyright Office under the 1909 Act: the use of Class E registrations for musical compositions, the deposit of copies, the distinction between unpublished and published works, and circulars explaining foreign protection (the United States was not then a member of the Berne Union). Handwritten annotations show the practical steps of remitting fees and sending copies. The presence of both Czech-language material and English official documents illustrates the bilingual world of many first- and second-generation immigrants.

## What the Documents Preserve

The envelope and its contents form a compact archive of one man’s creative and professional activity across two Czech-American communities and two decades. They record specific musical titles and registration numbers, document the mechanics of copyright registration in the 1920s–1940s, and preserve a Czech naturopathy text. Placed against the known history of Czech settlement in Dickinson and Scio, they illustrate how cultural expression, community life, and ordinary bureaucratic processes intersected in the lives of immigrants and their families.

Further research can continue through deposit-copy requests, local historical societies in Stark County, North Dakota, and Linn County, Oregon, and genealogical records.
